Architecture + experiment brief · adaptive_range_v1

Research the questions that can change the watchlist.

Before the market opens, forecast each stock’s daily trading range. Spend a limited research budget on factual questions that could alter the selected stocks. Verify the evidence, update the forecasts, and keep a replayable record of every decision.

What has been demonstrated: the harness executes, enforces controls, and replays correctly in scripted tests. Forecasting skill has not been established. The scripted forecaster deliberately keeps the historical baseline unchanged.

01

The decision we are trying to improve

Prediction task

Same-day relative range, before the open

At 09:00 Eastern, rank the provided stock group by predicted regular-session relative range. The highest k forecasts form the watchlist; k is configurable.

For one stock and one target session, let H be its highest regular-session price and L > 0 its lowest. Both are unknown at the cutoff. The realized range R is:

R=H−LL

A dimensionless ratio: 0.05 means 5%. It is not a standard deviation, direction forecast, or directly achievable trading return.

Why this target matters

A quiet close can hide a wide session

Illustrative prices: a stock opens at $100, trades between $95 and $105, and closes at $100.

105−9595=1095≈0.105263=10.5263%

Its open-to-close return is (100 − 100) / 100 = 0%, but its relative range is about 10.53%.

Researchers need a reason for uncertainty or repricing during the regular session. A large overnight opening gap alone does not establish a wide session range.

Why the scenario calculation is mean-preserving

Fix the current forecast while comparing this question’s answers. The quantities below are fixed scheduling assumptions; their mathematical consistency does not establish predictive calibration.

ms
Current nonnegative expected-range forecast for stock s.
pa
Probability of proposed answer a, conditional on useful resolution; nonnegative and summing to one.
ds,a
Proposed log-multiplicative shift for stock s under answer a.
bs,a
Applied shift after clipping d to [−1.5, 1.5] and multiplying by 0.5.
r, c
Estimated useful-resolution probability r in [0, 1], and positive reserved research cost c in dollars.
  1. Transform the proposed shifts.

    For the default B example, proposed shifts +1 and −1 lie within the clipping bounds. Halving them gives applied shifts +0.5 and −0.5. The natural exponential function, exp, converts these log shifts to positive multipliers.

  2. Build a positive normalizer for each affected stock.
    Zs=∑apaexp(bs,a)

    Each exponential is positive and the probabilities sum to one, so Zs > 0. For B, Z = 0.5 × exp(0.5) + 0.5 × exp(−0.5) = 1.1276259652.

  3. Normalize the answer-specific forecast.
    ms,a=msexp(bs,a)Zs

    B’s forecasts become 0.02 × exp(0.5) / 1.1276259652 = 0.0292423431 and 0.02 × exp(−0.5) / 1.1276259652 = 0.0107576569. A is unaffected and remains 0.025 in both scenarios.

  4. Verify the mean before using the scenarios.
    ∑apams,a=msZs∑apaexp(bs,a)=msZsZs=ms

    The first step substitutes the definition and factors out ms / Zs, which does not depend on the answer. The sum is exactly Zs by definition; its positivity permits cancellation. Numerically, 0.5 × 0.0292423431 + 0.5 × 0.0107576569 = 0.0200000000. A zero current mean also stays zero in every multiplicative scenario. Do not clip the normalized means afterward.

  5. Measure how learning could change selection.

    Let S0 be the currently selected top-k companies. For any full forecast vector x, define Tk(x) as the sum of its k largest entries (or all entries when fewer than k stocks are eligible). Let ma be the full vector under answer a.

    priority=rc∑apa[Tk(ma)−∑s∈S0ms,a]

    The bracket compares the best selection under that answer with retaining the original selection. It is nonnegative because retaining the original selection remains an available choice. Nonnegative answer weights and r, divided by positive c, preserve that sign.

    With one slot, only answer 1 replaces A: conditional advantage = 0.5 × (0.0292423431 − 0.025) + 0.5 × 0 = 0.0021211716. Useful-resolution weighting gives 0.8 × 0.0021211716 = 0.0016969373. Dividing by $0.10 gives 0.0169693726 ratio units per dollar, equivalent to 1.69693726 percentage points per dollar.

Why normalize? Without normalization, the same ±0.5 shifts would average to 0.0225525193, silently raising B’s 2% mean to 2.2553% before research. Mean preservation prevents this inflation. It does not establish that the answers, probabilities, shifts, or resolution estimates are correct.

Why a lower-score question can run first

The comparison smoke test was deliberately small

How the primary metric works

Fix one evaluated session with complete labels. Let k be the chosen watchlist size, S the selected set of k stocks, and O the set of k stocks with the largest realized ranges. For stock s, Rs is its realized relative range.

regret=1k∑s∈ORs−1k∑s∈SRs

Smaller is better. On the one scored smoke-test date, the oracle mean range was 3.94431918% and the selected mean was 3.23879445%. Subtraction gives 3.94431918 − 3.23879445 = 0.70552473 percentage points of regret, or a ratio of 0.0070552473. This is a descriptive fixture result, not an out-of-sample superiority finding.

Partial and missing labels are reported explicitly. Partial-coverage scores use the labelled eligible universe, preserve selected positions, and give missing selected labels zero contribution; they must not be interpreted as complete-day regret.

07

The research claim we can test next

The candidate contribution is question-specific research allocation for a range watchlist. Agent count, budget awareness, and attention to the top-k boundary already have precedents. The open empirical question is whether our answer-sensitive priorities improve selection at a matched cost.

Established in this version

A usable experiment harness

  • Bounded planning and local research.
  • Verified-card interfaces and targeted repair.
  • Evidence-gated numerical updates.
  • Explicit costs, source timing, aliases, and memory isolation.
  • Comparable policies, saved decisions, and exact recorded replay.
Needed for a forecasting claim

A larger, genuinely informative study

  • More dates and events with reliable point-in-time sources and complete labels.
  • A model/date protocol addressing historical knowledge.
  • A real-model headroom test against strong historical baselines.
  • Held-out comparisons of allocation policies at binding budgets.
  • Independent evidence audits and dependence-aware uncertainty estimates.
A 60-second explanation for teammates

“We built a premarket research harness to rank stocks by their expected intraday high-to-low range. Every stock starts with a historical baseline. An orchestrator proposes factual questions, and code allocates a limited budget based on how learning an answer might change the watchlist. General workers inspect frozen evidence, a verifier checks their cards, and accepted new information can inform forecast updates. We have tested execution, controls, and replay. The current scripted fixture keeps numerical forecasts unchanged, so it has not established an accuracy advantage. The next experiment is whether targeted research improves watchlist quality over simpler allocation at the same cost.”
